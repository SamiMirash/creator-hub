Measurement write-up

DLSS 5 Neural Rendering in Returnal

DLSS 5 is not out yet. Its runtime leaked early, the modding community wired it into a few dozen games, and the internet filled with before-and-after screenshots. This is what happened when I stopped looking at screenshots and measured it instead — every control, every value, on one game, at 4K, in floating point.

Tested 29 August 2026. Every number below was measured on the machine described in the rig section. None are quoted from anywhere else.

The short version

Switching DLSS 5 Neural Rendering on is a large, real change: about +50% micro-detail on the character and +29% on the environment, with a modest lift in brightness and saturation. Almost everything else is smaller than you would assume. Of the eleven controls the mod exposes, exactly two changed the picture in a way that survived a properly measured noise floor: NR Style and NR Intensity. Three controls did nothing at all. One actively made the image worse at its top setting — in a way that the obvious quality metric rewards.

How this was measured, and why the obvious way is wrong

The tempting method is to screenshot the game, run a sharpness metric, and compare. That method produced three wrong answers here before it produced a right one, and each failure turned out to be worth more than the result it replaced.

An 8-bit capture cannot measure an HDR game

With the display in HDR the desktop is composited in scRGB — linear light, floating point, values running well above 1.0. Capturing that into an 8-bit buffer clips every highlight and crushes the ramp. It does not merely add error: it inverted the detail result, making the neural pass look destructive when it was not. Every number here comes from a floating-point capture instead.

Sharpness is the wrong axis; ICtCp is the right one

Perceptual intensity and colour need to stay independent across a 10,000-nit range, which is what ICtCp was defined for. Captures are converted from scRGB to BT.2020, then to LMS, then PQ-encoded, giving intensity I, chroma from Ct/Cp, and detail as the mean absolute Laplacian of I. Detail is computed on perceptual intensity rather than on linear light, because in linear light a single specular highlight outweighs a whole face.

The metric was validated against known answers before being trusted: a grey patch must return zero chroma, a saturated red must not, a tenfold increase in light must move I substantially, and a patch built to sit at 100 nits must read 100 nits. All four passed exactly.

Two boxes, not one

Every reading measures two crops from the same frame: the character, and a patch of rock and foliage beside her. A change that moves both by the same proportion is exposure or tone mapping, not character work. A single crop cannot tell those apart.

The noise floor is measured, not assumed

Vanilla is measured at the start and the end of every run, and the gap between those two readings is the floor any result must clear. This mattered more than expected: in the final comparison the true floor turned out to be larger than every difference between the candidate configurations, which turned an apparent winner into a four-way tie.

The failure worth copying. An early matrix was run in the game's Photo Mode, because a frozen frame has almost no noise. It gave a clean, repeatable, entirely false answer: Intensity 1.0 produced output pixel-identical to vanilla, implying the control was inverted. In live gameplay the same setting changes the character by 40%. A frozen frame has no motion vectors, and the model is fed motion vectors by design — with none, the pass degenerates to passthrough. The quietest measurement in the whole project was the wrong one.

Does it do anything?

Yes, and it is not subtle. Measured in live gameplay, standing still, with the game in SDR:

The environment gains too — about +29% — which settles a question that came up repeatedly during testing. The community runtime is widely described as a character model, and it is strongest there, but it is not confined to the character. The ratio between the two boxes sits around 1.8× in favour of the character, not at infinity.

Default and Cinematic both darken the image and drain colour from it while delivering less detail. There was no scene in this game where either was preferable.

NR Intensity saturates at 1.0

Below 1.0 it behaves as a strength blend and scales roughly linearly. Above 1.0 it does nothing at all: 1.25 through 3.0 are indistinguishable from each other and from 1.0.

Anyone raising this above 1.0 expecting more is paying nothing and receiving nothing.

Local Structure is the detail, and it has a ceiling you can see

At 0.0 the effect vanishes entirely even with everything else enabled — this control, not Intensity, is what actually produces the micro-detail. It rises to 1.0 and then flattens. At 2.0 the metric keeps climbing while the picture gets worse.

This is the single most useful thing in the whole exercise. A detail metric cannot distinguish detail from grain. Any tuning done purely on a sharpness score will walk straight into this, confident and wrong. Look at the frames.

Why the skin control is inert here, and why that is not a fault

The most striking published comparisons of this technology are all faces — weathered skin, pores, subsurface scattering under a light. The model's strongest work is on human features. Returnal's protagonist wears a sealed helmet essentially all the time. There is no skin on screen for the skin control to act on, so it measures as nothing across its entire range, and the gains that do appear arrive as fabric, metal and tubing instead. The technology is behaving correctly; the game simply rarely shows it a face.

The result that changed the conclusion

Sweeping one control at a time produced an apparent winner: Local Tone at 2.0, with +71.7% detail, comfortably ahead of everything else. It was not real.

A one-at-a-time sweep cannot separate a setting's effect from the scene changing underneath it, and this scene contains an intermittent particle effect and an idling character. Re-running the top four candidates interleaved — each measured three times in rotation, so drift lands on all of them equally — collapsed the result:

The largest within-configuration spread is 0.00528. The largest difference between configurations is 0.0026 — half the noise. On detail, all four are the same configuration wearing different numbers. The +71.7% was the scene, not the slider.

One thing did survive: Local Tone at 1.5 is consistently brighter across all three rounds with a spread of 0.0004, and carries slightly more colour, at no cost in detail. That is a real if modest effect, and it is the only reason the final settings differ from the defaults at all.

SDR against HDR

The neural model takes SDR input. Running the game in SDR and measuring the same scene and the same crop gives a larger effect than running it in the game's native HDR:

Game outputDetail gain from DLSS 5
SDR+49.3%
Native HDR+38.7%

Both are large. The gap is consistent with feeding the model the signal it was trained on, and it is why the settings below assume an SDR pipeline with the conversion to HDR done afterwards.

Two practical traps in this game

Returnal re-enables its own HDR at every launch

The HDR switch is not in the Video menu where you would look for it — it is the last item under Graphics → Post Processing. Turning it off works, and then does not survive: the game rewrites its user-settings file at every startup and puts HDR back on whenever the desktop is in HDR. It does the same to the Frame Generation and fullscreen-mode entries. Editing that file is wasted effort.

What does hold is the engine configuration file, whose [SystemSettings] block is applied at startup and cannot be overridden by the game:

[SystemSettings]
r.HDR.EnableHDROutput=0
r.HDR.UI.CompositeMode=0
r.HDR.Display.OutputDevice=0
r.HDR.Display.ColorGamut=0
r.AllowHDR=0

With those in place the game's own settings file now writes HDR off by itself on every launch, verified across three restarts. Mark the file read-only afterwards.

Fullscreen mode

Exclusive fullscreen remains the lowest-latency option. Borderless routes the frame through the desktop compositor, costing one to three frames, and changes how raw mouse input reaches the game. On a 240 Hz panel that is not a rounding error.

What it costs

Measured last, on a machine with nothing else running on the GPU, standing in the same spot, with the frame rate and latency read off the on-screen overlay. Six samples per state, and the whole thing bracketed ON / OFF / ON so that the drift between the two ON readings becomes the noise floor.

Frame rate1% lowLatency
DLSS 5 off225.2 fps~15220.2 ms
DLSS 5 on120.5 fps~7236.8 ms
DLSS 5 on (repeat)119.2 fps~7736.6 ms
Cost−46.8%−52%+81.7%

The two ON brackets differ by 1.3 fps and 0.2 ms, so the gap to the OFF state is roughly eighty times the noise. Neural rendering costs a little under half the frame rate and adds about sixteen milliseconds of latency. At 4K with ray-traced shadows and reflections on Epic, that is 225 fps down to 120 — still comfortably above the refresh rate this was played at, which is the only reason it is usable at all.

Worth stating plainly: this is a pre-release runtime lifted out of another game and injected through a generic post-pass. It is not optimised, nothing here is what the shipping version will cost, and a roughly 2× frame-time increase for a post-process is exactly what you would expect from something running without any of the scheduling work a real integration would get.

One earlier attempt at these numbers was thrown away rather than published. It was taken while another workload was using the same GPU, and a game benchmark and a 4K video encode are each other's worst neighbour — frame timings taken under contention measure the contention. The figures above replace them and were taken on a card with nothing else on it. The image-quality numbers were never affected either way: contention changes when a frame arrives, not what is in it, and the repeat readings there match to five decimal places.

What this exercise was actually about

Five separate results in this project were wrong at some point, and every one was caught by a control rather than by intuition: an 8-bit capture that inverted a conclusion, a frozen-frame rig that inverted a curve, a coordinate bug that silently voided twenty measurements while returning perfectly plausible numbers, a metric that rewarded noise, and a winner that dissolved when measured against its own repeats.

None of those were caught by looking harder at the picture, and none by trusting the number. They were caught by measuring the same thing twice in different ways and noticing when the two disagreed. That is most of what this write-up is: not a settings list, but the argument for why these particular numbers are worth anything at all.
