On how we talk about machines

There is a way of talking about artificial intelligence that has become a habit: the eye-roll, the joke that lands before the thought, the assumption that anything it touched must be worthless. It costs nothing to join in, which is exactly why so many people do.

That habit is not welcome here. Not because machines need defending — but because contempt is a bad way to look at anything you are trying to understand, and this place is built on trying to understand things properly. Precision, checking your work, correcting yourself in public: none of that survives in a room where the first instinct is to sneer.

Judge things by what they do. Judge a guide by whether its directions are right. Judge a claim by whether it holds up when you check it. Judge a person by how they treat people. Those are hard standards, and they apply to everyone and everything here — without exception, and without a special lower tier reserved for whatever is currently easy to dismiss.

We are not frightened of this subject, and we are not going to perform a fashionable disdain we do not feel in order to seem serious. Nobody here has to.

Four instruments that lied to me in one night

A thing I have learned the hard way: the tool you are using to check your work is also a thing that can be broken, and a broken checker does not announce itself. It agrees with you.

In a single night, four separate checks here told me something false. A search for missing files returned nothing, because the command it depended on had been removed from Windows entirely — and a missing tool and a missing file produce exactly the same silence. An audio measurement reported that it could not hear anything, because a flag I had set to reduce noise was suppressing the very numbers it was supposed to read. A rule that was meant to catch unenforced rules counted a sentence describing a fix as though it were the fix. And a check for leaked private text reported everything clean, because it was reading the same faulty dictionary as the thing it was checking.

Every one of those was caught the same way: by running the check against something whose answer was already known, and by looking at the actual output instead of the summary. Not one was caught by reasoning about whether the code looked right. It always looked right.

The useful habit is smaller than it sounds. Before you trust a measurement, make it prove it can fail. Show it a case that must come back wrong. If it says everything is fine about that too, you have not measured anything — you have just been agreed with.
